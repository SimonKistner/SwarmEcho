Which PPO combination learns fastest?

Eleven runs · one shared 4,000-map dataset · one fixed held-out validation set

Recommendation: 6000 envs / 50 steps / 3 epochs / 5 minibatches

It achieved the highest observed validation success, 75.45%, and reached a useful high-performance threshold sooner than every other run: 70% in 2h 06m. It also unlocked the seven-agent target budget first, in 2h 06m. This is the strongest tested choice for your wall-clock objective; it is not evidence of a universal optimum.

What the four numbers actually control

For env count N, horizon T, epochs E and minibatches M, each rollout collects B = N × T environment transitions. Each optimizer minibatch contains approximately B / M transitions, and each rollout performs E × M optimizer steps. These counts describe environment transitions; the agent dimension and active-agent masks add work inside each transition.

The model is recurrent. Minibatches preserve environment sequences of length T; hidden state carries across rollout boundaries, while gradient backpropagation is limited to the sampled sequence. Therefore T affects both value bootstrapping and recurrent learning context. M changes gradient-update count and minibatch size as well as memory requirements. It is a learning parameter, not merely a way to fit the GPU.

PPO deliberately reuses a rollout through multiple epochs of minibatch optimization; that is the algorithmic reason E and M interact. See the original PPO paper.

1. Fewer minibatches was the clearest controlled improvement

Measured contrast: 4000/100/4/20 → 4000/100/4/5 changes only minibatch count. Best validation rose from 54.74% to 73.86%. At the same 180M environment steps, the scores were 54.74% versus 65.23%; this is not just a throughput gain. Last logged throughput increased from 11.2k to 18.6k SPS.

Mechanism: minibatches became four times larger, 20k → 80k transitions, while sequential optimizer steps per rollout fell from 80 → 20. This combines more efficient larger computations with fewer successive parameter changes on a fixed batch. The experiment establishes that the combined effect helped; it does not isolate larger gradients from fewer optimizer steps.

The baseline also paid for the initial pool build. Removing that setup disadvantage still leaves a substantial gap: measured from W&B initialization, time to 50% was about 2h 02m versus 1h 16m.

2. More environments helped only with an appropriate optimization balance

Measured extremes: 16000/25/1/10 collected 504M steps at about 30.7k SPS, versus the winner’s 344.7M at 21.2k SPS. Yet its best validation was only 63.29%, its last was 51.55%, and it never unlocked budget 7. More experience per second did not compensate for this combination’s learning behavior.

Controlled lower-end contrast: doubling envs from 1000 to 2000 with T=50, E=12 and M=5 improved peak validation from 34.77% to 39.70% and throughput from 3.6k to 5.4k SPS. Increasing parallelism was beneficial within that regime, but neither configuration became competitive.

Interpretation: a larger rollout can provide more varied experience, but collects more samples before the next policy refresh when T stays fixed. At fixed total batch size, more envs instead means shorter per-env trajectories. The high-env run here also changed T and E, so the results do not prove that 16k envs alone is bad. They reject 16k + 25 steps + one epoch as the best tested combination.

3. Small batches with twelve epochs did not transfer from G01

The two low-env finalists from the G01 proxy peaked at 34.77% and 39.70% on G03 and both remained at virtual budget 3. Their batches were only 50k and 100k transitions, versus the winner’s 300k, while they reused every sample twelve times rather than three.

That means 1,200 and 600 optimizer steps per million environment transitions, versus 50 for the winner: 24× and 12× as many small gradient updates per unit of fresh experience. They collected only 59M and 89M steps before cancellation. Their median logged approximate KL after 10M steps was about 0.019 and 0.018, versus 0.0046 for the winner, consistent with larger policy changes per rollout.

Likely explanation: repeatedly fitting a small, less varied rollout is an unfavorable trade on thousands of maps: it consumes time that could collect new layouts and outcomes, and may move the policy too far on the current data. The curve and KL support this explanation; they do not prove overfitting, a numerical failure, or that twelve epochs would fail with a much larger batch.

4. A longer horizon helped one matched pair; “shorter is faster” is not a rule

At N=4000, E=4 and M=5, increasing T from 50 to 100 raised the peak from 68.89% to 73.86%. The 100-step run reached 70% in 2h 32m and budget 7 in 3h 44m; the 50-step run reached neither. It also had higher logged throughput, 18.6k versus 16.0k SPS.

This change doubled rollout and minibatch sizes while halving the number of policy-update cycles per collected transition. Longer recurrent sequences and fewer rollout boundaries are plausible benefits, but the experiment does not separate them from batch-size and optimizer-frequency effects. The winner still uses T=50, so this finding is specific to the surrounding combination.

Likewise, the two 25-step runs both regressed substantially after their peaks: 8000/25/2/10 fell from 66.41% to 47.00%, and 16000/25/1/10 from 63.29% to 51.55%. Shorter credit-assignment sequences and more reliance on the critic at rollout cuts are plausible contributors, not demonstrated causes. Bootstrapping is the normal bias/variance trade described by generalized advantage estimation, rather than inherently a defect.

5. The promising region balances fresh data and reuse

The winner uses a 300k rollout, 60k-transition minibatches and 15 optimizer steps per rollout. The consistent runner-up uses 400k, 80k and 20. Both combine substantial batches with three or four reuse passes and relatively few minibatches.

A useful interaction contrast is 4000/100/4/5 versus 4000/100/2/10: both collect 400k transitions and perform 20 optimizer steps per rollout. Four passes over 80k minibatches outperformed two passes over 40k minibatches in time to 70% (2h 32m versus 3h 46m) and peak score (73.86% versus 71.26%). Equal optimizer-step count does not imply equivalent learning.

The eight-env-thousand variants tell a similar story without isolating a single cause: 8000/50/2/5 was faster in SPS than 8000/50/4/10 and finished better, but took longer to first reach 70%. Changing epochs and minibatches together changes the entire learning trajectory. The current evidence supports moderate reuse with large minibatches, not simply minimizing or maximizing any one knob.

Other findings that affect how to read these runs

Budget 7 measures exposure, not mastery

8000/25/2/10 unlocked budget 7 in 2h 19m, only about 13 minutes behind the winner, yet finished at 47% validation. The winner’s first completed training episodes labelled as requiring seven agents appeared around 2h 08m, corroborating the gate time. At cancellation, however, its rolling training success for that exact estimator category was only 0.8%; the runner-up’s was 0.6%. These are adaptive-training category windows, not independent held-out success rates. Unlocking the category does not establish that the hardest category is solved.

Inspection cadence is part of the experiment

Inspection uses 100-update spacing and validation 50-update spacing. With a 300k rollout, the winner can advance the curriculum roughly every 30M steps; a 400k rollout waits roughly 40M steps. After its initial inspection, the winner passed each of the next five inspection gates, progressing at 30.3M, 60.3M, 90.3M, 120.3M and 150.3M. Its budget-7 time is therefore partly a consequence of the configured check cadence. That is legitimate for the current end-to-end objective, but not a pure measure of latent policy quality. No frequencies were changed for this analysis.

Slow experience collection also delayed adaptive map sampling

Adaptive map weighting waits until every one of the 3,600 training maps has a full 100-outcome window. Neither twelve-epoch run filled even one map’s window by cancellation; both remained in map-sampling warmup. The winner activated map weighting at about 2h 09m, the runner-up at 2h 16m. Thus the PPO choices changed when another enabled training mechanism became active. This is an interaction, not a configuration mismatch; the fast 16k run activated it earlier still and nevertheless underperformed.

Higher overall success has not solved deployment efficiency

At its record checkpoint the winner visually found the target in 99.4% of validation episodes, delivered target information in 86.37%, and completed chain success in 75.45%. The remaining gap is increasingly downstream of finding the target. Its final training diagnostics also show about 2.54 unused agents per successful episode under the contributor metric, with decommissioning almost absent. Faster PPO learning is not evidence that recruitment efficiency has converged.

Keep the record and the retention result separate

The winner’s record checkpoint scored 75.45%, but its last validation was 71.45%. The runner-up ended at its own record, 73.86%. The winner’s record had a nominal 95% episode-level interval of 74.60–76.28%, versus 72.99–74.71% for the runner-up’s record. Those intervals omit training-seed variability and repeated checkpoint selection; they do not prove an across-seed ranking.

What I would carry forward

Use 6000/50/3/5 as the current G03 wall-clock candidate and retain 4000/100/4/5 as the consistency reference. Under a record-success objective the former wins both the highest observed score and the quickest arrival at the high-performance region. Under a final-policy or stability objective the latter deserves equal attention. Preserve their best validation checkpoints where available; cancellation did not run normal finalization.

I would retire the tested 1k/2k + 12-epoch and 16k/25/1 configurations from this G03 comparison. The 8k/25/2 configuration is useful evidence that early curriculum progress can mislead, rather than a strong continuation candidate. Before calling any combination universally best, the important confirmation would be the two leading choices under additional training seeds or longer equal wall-time budgets, not another broad parameter grid. No training settings or experiments were changed or submitted for this report.

Data, timing and limits

Fetched 11,313 W&B history rows and 220 completed validations across all eleven runs on 30 October 2026. For each run, the fetched history-row count matches W&B’s recorded history count; every validation score and step matches the PFSS validation journal. Recorded step increments match the stated PPO batch sizes, so there is no evidence of silent OOM environment reductions in these trajectories. All selections are byte-equivalent after canonical JSON serialization, and every validation journal has identity 1779bcb5e44b558ac65f4ebc7b03cf16c6f66e527d980f0c4034ccf291f9b99b.

All runs used seed 456789, the same 3,600 training / 400 held-out map split, gamma 0.99, lambda 0.95, learning rate 0.0003, recurrent actor/critic, full seven-slot recruitment, adaptive training maps/cells and the virtual target curriculum. Comparing recorded configs reveals only the four PPO changes and logging identities. The hardware context is the supplied A100 cluster setup; per-phase GPU/CPU utilization was not profiled, so larger minibatch speed explanations remain mechanisms to investigate, not a measured utilization result.

Time zero is the training process’s budget_started clock: CPU setup, pool preparation, initialization, training, evaluation and checkpoint overhead after that point count. It excludes earlier queueing and container startup. W&B logs perf/wall_seconds before evaluation but completes a validation row afterward. To avoid crediting a score before it was measured, this report uses _runtime + median(perf/wall_seconds − _runtime), with the offset estimated only on rows without inspection or validation. Offset variation is under 3 seconds within every run; event times are rounded to minutes. This is an approximate completion clock, not a separately instrumented phase timer.

The baseline’s W&B initialization offset was about 23.1 minutes, versus 3.3–4.6 minutes for the other runs, consistent with its cold pool build. The plots and all-eleven table retain that real cost. The baseline column in the first comparison table explicitly excludes this initial building/setup time, and the controlled minibatch contrast also gives a post-W&B-init comparison. SPS is the last logged cumulative rate, whose clock starts later than total setup; it is not a phase-isolated kernel benchmark.

Threshold times are the first observed completed validation at or above the threshold. Because validation happens every 50 PPO updates, sampling resolution differs with batch size. No interpolation is used, and peak comparisons have different numbers of validation opportunities (9–32 per run). The seven-agent event is the inspection gate’s recorded 6→7 transition; the next rollout can then sample that budget.
